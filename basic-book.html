BLIP BASIC

CHAPTER ONE

YOUR FIRST PROGRAM

BASIC lets you give the computer a short list of instructions. Type a line, press RETURN, then ask BLIP to run the lines in order.

You do not need a whole game in your head to begin. Give the machine one small job and see what happens. A greeting today, a bouncing ball tomorrow: both begin with you telling the computer what to do next.

10 PRINT "HELLO WORLD"
20 GOTO 10
  1. Type 10 PRINT "HELLO WORLD" and press RETURN.
  2. Type 20 GOTO 10 and press RETURN.
  3. Type RUN to start.
  4. Press ESCAPE to stop and return to BASIC.
LINE NUMBERSThe number at the start tells BASIC where each instruction belongs. Leave gaps—10, 20, 30—so you can add a line later.

Type LIST to see the program. Enter a line again to replace it. NEW deletes the program; CLEAR resets variables and the screen but keeps its lines.

Try replacing HELLO WORLD with your own message. The computer will repeat it with heroic enthusiasm until you press ESCAPE. You have just changed a program and tested the result. Keep that habit; it takes you a long way.

NUMBERS, CHOICES & LOOPS

CHAPTER TWO

NUMBERS, CHOICES & LOOPS

A variable is a named place to keep a value. Use words to make a program easier to follow.

Think of a scoreboard with room for more than scores. It can remember a player’s position, how many lives remain, or whether the monster has noticed you. Assigning a new value changes what the program remembers.

10 PLAYER_X=150
20 LIVES=3
30 PRINT "LIVES";LIVES

MAKE A DECISION

10 IF LIVES=0 THEN 100
20 PRINT "PLAYING"
30 GOTO 10
100 PRINT "GAME OVER"

REPEAT A GROUP

10 FOR STAR=1 TO 8
20 PRINT "*";
30 NEXT STAR
40 PRINT

GOTO jumps to a line. GOSUB calls a numbered helper; RETURN comes back. A named SUB, over the page, is easier to read. Put multiple statements on a line with colons. IF … THEN controls the rest of that line.

EXPRESSIONS

+ − * / MOD, comparisons = <> < > <= >=, and AND/OR.

FUNCTIONS

ABS, INT, SQR, SIN, COS, RND, LEN, STR$, COLOR.

BLIP BASIC stores source and string values in capitals. Commands and variable names are case-insensitive.

3
NUMBERS, CHOICES & LOOPS

NAME YOUR ROUTINES.

A SUB is a routine with a name and its own values. Call it by name; BASIC comes back when it reaches END SUB.

Imagine shouting “paint a stripe!” to a very literal assistant. The routine is its recipe; the parameters tell it where to paint and which colour to use. Write the recipe once, then call for as many stripes as you need.

10 FOR ROW=0 TO 4
20   CALL STRIPE(ROW*80,"BLIP_CYAN")
30 NEXT ROW
40 END
100 SUB STRIPE(Y,C$)
110   FILL_RECT(0,Y,640,40,C$)
120 END SUB

CALL IT EITHER WAY

CALL STRIPE(0,"BLIP_RED") or just STRIPE 0,"BLIP_RED". Normal flow steps over a SUB; only a call runs it.

ITS OWN VALUES

Parameters and names after LOCAL belong to the call and are put back afterwards. Every other name is shared with the program.

LEAVE EARLYEXIT SUB returns at once. A SUB may call itself: each call keeps its own parameters.

A MOD B is the remainder after dividing, always from 0 up to B: X MOD 640 wraps a position around the screen.

NUMBERS, CHOICES & LOOPS

BLOCKS, LOOPS AND DATA.

When a choice needs more than one line, end the IF line with THEN and close the block with END IF.

10 IF LIVES=0 THEN
20   PRINT "GAME OVER"
30 ELSEIF LIVES=1 THEN
40   PRINT "LAST LIFE"
50 ELSE
60   PRINT "PLAYING"
70 END IF

REPEAT WHILE

WHILE X<100 … WEND repeats while the test holds. EXIT FOR leaves a FOR loop early.

A ONE-LINE FUNCTION

DEF FNSQUARE(N)=N*N, then use FNSQUARE(4) in any expression.

KEEP TABLES IN DATA

10 FOR I=1 TO 3:READ NAME$(I),SPEED(I):NEXT I
20 DATA "RYUKA",1.1, "BRUTUS",0.8, "KESTREL",1.36

READ takes the next DATA item; RESTORE 900 starts again at line 900. For text there are MID$(S$,START,LENGTH), LEFT$, RIGHT$, VAL, ASC and CHR$.

BLIP BASIC

CHAPTER THREE

READ INPUT. UPDATE. DRAW.

A game runs one frame at a time. Check the controls, change the game state, and draw a new picture.

A moving paddle is a sequence of still pictures with a little arithmetic between them. Each trip around the loop asks where it should be now. Draw those answers quickly enough and your eyes supply the movement. No tiny stagehands required.

10 CLEAR("BLIP_BLACK")
20 IF KEY_HELD("LEFT") THEN PLAYER_X=PLAYER_X-4
30 IF KEY_HELD("RIGHT") THEN PLAYER_X=PLAYER_X+4
40 DRAW_TEXTURE("/TAPE/BOUNCER/ASSETS/PADDLE.PNG",PLAYER_X,360,100,16)
50 WAIT 16
60 GOTO 10

KEYBOARD CONTROLS

KEY_HELD("LEFT") stays true while a key is down; KEY_PRESSED reads a new press and KEY_ACTIVE either. "SPACE" or "FIRE" is fire, "BUTTON2" is Z, and letters A–G, I, J and K work too. INKEY$ returns a pressed key as text.

WAIT 16 pauses about one frame at 60 frames per second. END finishes the program. ESCAPE and EXIT GAME leave a running game.

COORDINATESThe screen is 640 × 400 pixels, a high-resolution Amiga’s. The origin is at the upper left; x grows right and y grows down. CANVAS(W,H) picks another size: Bubbler keeps its arcade arena, 624 × 600.

READ INPUT. UPDATE. DRAW.

EVERY GAME, THE SAME SHAPE.

Open any tape and you find the same plan. Learn it once and you can find your way round all nine games.

LINES 1–9
What the game is, its keys and its screens. The REM GAME: comment identifies its controls.
LINE 10 · SETUP
Sizes, speeds and colours, each with a name. Near the end of setup, the music starts and the first screen is chosen; exact line numbers vary by tape.
LINE 100 · MAIN LOOP
Read the keys, update the screen we are on, draw it, WAIT 0, and round again.
LINE 1000 ON
A section for each screen and each thing in the game, under a REM --- heading.
130 IF STATE$="TITLE" THEN
131   UPDATE_TITLE
132 ELSEIF STATE$="PLAY" THEN
133   UPDATE_PLAY
134 ELSE
135   UPDATE_OVER
136 END IF

Each screen is a pair of routines: UPDATE_PLAY changes the game and DRAW_PLAY paints it. START_GAME sets up a new game. A routine’s body is indented two spaces, and a line holds one statement.

Read the main loop like a contents page. When a name catches your eye, visit that routine, then come back. You can understand the match before you understand every bounce.

READ INPUT. UPDATE. DRAW.

NAMES THAT TELL.

A good name says what a routine does, so a routine that calls others reads like a list of jobs.

4210 SUB UPDATE_PLAY
4220   MOVE_PLAYERS
4230   MOVE_TOYS
4240   MOVE_BUBBLES
4250   HURT_PLAYERS
4260 END SUB

VERB FIRST

MOVE_, DRAW_, START_, FIRE, POP_CHAIN: say what happens. The plural moves them all; the singular moves one, MOVE_TOY I.

QUESTIONS AS FN

A DEF FN answers yes or no: FNIN_PLAY(P), FNBLOCK(C,R). Read it aloud in an IF.

THE SAME NAMES IN RUST

The arcade games are written in Rust to the same plan, with update_play, draw_play and start_game. Read a tape, then its Rust game, and the parts line up.

SAY WHY, NOT WHATA REM earns its place with a rule or a reason, such as “A JUMP IS TOLD BEFORE IT HAPPENS”. The code already says what it does.

THE ARCADE DOESN’T FIT

An arcade game is tuned to its own cabinet. Bringing it home means rebuilding it around a smaller memory, a different screen, new controls and a very different sound chip.

THE HARD PART IS THE TRADE-OFF

Fewer colours may change a scene; slower drawing can change the timing; one-button controls can change a move. A faithful screenshot is not enough if the game no longer feels right. Good conversions simplify detail, protect the essential motion, and keep the controls immediate.

Pause between notes and effects.

Stop the current music track.

SOUND & MUSIC

WRITE A TUNE.

TUNE opens a small tracker: four chip voices, each playing down sixteen steps.

  1. Enter TUNE.
  2. Choose a NOTE, from C2 to B5.
  3. Click a cell to put the note there; click again to clear it. The rows are the voices: square, pulse, triangle and noise.
  4. ▶ adds a pattern and PAT moves between them, up to 32. BPM sets the speed, from 50 to 240.
  5. PLAY listens and STOP stops.
  6. Name it, for example /USER/MUSIC/THEME.TUNE, and SAVE.
80 PLAY_MUSIC "/USER/MUSIC/THEME"

SAVE keeps the notes in THEME.TUNE, to edit later, and the sound in THEME.WAV, which PLAY_MUSIC plays round and round. Change line 80 in your copy of a game and it plays your theme.

Every tune on a tape has its notes beside it: TUNE /TAPE/SERPENT/ASSETS/SLITHER.XM opens them to read, play and save as your own.

FROM A TRACKERIMPORT XM brings in the notes of a MilkyTracker module: its first four channels and up to 32 patterns, at its tempo. BLIP’s chip voices play them.

A tape carries its game’s pictures, effects and tunes in one folder, ready to use.

Stretch an image; optionally multiply it by a tint. A negative width mirrors it, so a figure turns to face the other way.

REPAINT A GAME.

A tape’s pictures are read-only, but your own copy of a game is yours to repaint.

  1. Enter LOAD /TAPE/BUBBLER.BLIP, then RUN.
  2. Press ESCAPE. BASIC copies the game to /USER/BUBBLER/BUBBLER.BLIP and its pictures to /USER/BUBBLER/ASSETS, then opens the copy in the editor.

EDIT FILE opens a full-screen source editor on the monitor, for example EDIT /USER/MY_BOUNCER.BLIP. It opens an existing file or creates a new one under /USER.

ARROW KEYS

Move the caret. Up and down keep it as near as possible to the same column.

HOME / END

Move to the start or end of the line.

TYPE / RETURN

Insert uppercase text at the caret. RETURN splits the line.

BACKSPACE / DEL

Remove text. At a line boundary, the line joins its neighbour.

TAB

Accept the dimmed completion suggestion, or insert spaces.

ESCAPE

Close the editor and return to the BASIC prompt.

Every edit is saved to the open /USER file. Keywords and strings are highlighted; dimmed completion text joins the source only when you press TAB.

APPENDIX A · THE RALLY CODE WALK

TWO PADDLES. ONE PROGRAM.

Let’s lift the lid on Rally. Everything in this walkthrough comes from /TAPE/RALLY.BLIP: a complete game built from numbers, choices, and a very persistent loop.

LOAD /TAPE/RALLY.BLIP
SAVE MY_RALLY
SOURCE
EDIT /USER/MY_RALLY.BLIP

Load the tape and save your own copy before tinkering. SOURCE lets you read it; EDIT opens your copy. Keep REM GAME: RALLY so BLIP knows which controls belong to it.

YOUR MAP OF THE COURT

Lines 10–90 set the rules. Lines 100–180 run the frame loop. The 1000s draw the title and choose players; the 2000s prepare a match and serve. The 3000s run play, the 4000s move paddles, and the 5000s handle the ball.

The 7000s award points, the 8000s finish the match, and the 9000s read keys and draw the court. Follow the routine names as signposts. We will take this tour one bounce at a time.

22
INSIDE RALLY · SETUP AND TIME

GIVE THE GAME ITS RULES.

Setup is the game’s control panel. Line 20 sets WIN=7. Lines 30–45 set paddle size, court positions, and ball size. Line 50 gives the player a speed of 320 pixels per second and the CPU 170. The machine gets a sporting chance, not a teleport button.

55 SERVE_SPEED=360:SPEED_UP=20:TOP_SPEED=600
80 STATE$="TITLE"
90 LAST_TIME=TIME()

The three speed settings describe the opening serve, the increase after each paddle hit, and the ceiling. STATE$ remembers which screen is running. LAST_TIME remembers when the previous frame began.

110 NOW=TIME()
111 DT=CLAMP(NOW-LAST_TIME,0.0001,0.1)
112 LAST_TIME=NOW
120 READ_KEYS

DT is elapsed time in seconds. Movement uses speed times DT, so a paddle travels roughly the same distance per second at different frame rates. The clamp limits a single update to a tenth of a second after a long pause.

Lines 130–160 choose an update routine and a drawing routine using STATE$. Then WAIT 0 yields a frame and GOTO 110 starts the next lap. That is the engine room.

23
INSIDE RALLY · INPUT AND SERVING

WHO’S HOLDING THE PADDLE?

READ_KEYS, at line 9010, gathers input once per frame. W/S or the arrows control the left paddle; I/K control the right. KEY_ACTIVE supplies movement, while KEY_PRESSED notices a fresh press for choosing players and serving.

On the title screen, UPDATE_TITLE checks the right paddle first. Move it and PLAYERS=2; otherwise a left-paddle press or fire chooses one player. One routine, two ways to start a rivalry.

START_GAME clears both scores, clears the CPU’s aiming error, and randomly picks a serve direction. RESET_FOR_SERVE centres the paddles and ball, stops the ball, and restores its opening speed.

3030   IF FIRE OR LEFT_MOVED OR RIGHT_MOVED THEN
3040     LAUNCH
3050     STATE$="PLAY"
3060   END IF

The serve screen lets you position the paddles before play. A fresh movement press or fire calls LAUNCH. That routine picks a small random angle and uses COS and SIN to split BALL_SPEED into horizontal and vertical velocity.

Now STATE$ says PLAY. The next update moves a live ball; the drawing code shows the same court without the serve prompt. The match is underway.

24
INSIDE RALLY · PADDLES AND CPU

A RIVAL WITH LIMITS.

4030   LPAD_Y=LPAD_Y+PAD_SPEED*DT*(LEFT_DOWN-LEFT_UP)

This line packs a whole control scheme into one expression. Down contributes 1, up contributes −1, and neither contributes 0. Holding both cancels out. Multiplying by speed and elapsed time turns that direction into a distance.

In two-player mode the right paddle uses the same recipe. In one-player mode the CPU moves only during PLAY. When the ball travels right, it aims its paddle centre at the ball centre, with AI_ERR nudging the target off perfection.

4130     RPAD_Y=RPAD_Y+CLAMP(TARGET-RPAD_Y,-REACH,REACH)

REACH is how far the CPU may travel this frame. The clamp stops it jumping straight to the target. When the ball travels away, the CPU drifts towards HOME_Y at half speed. It has a plan, but it still has to get there.

Lines 4150–4160 clamp both paddles inside the court. Because a paddle’s Y value describes its top edge, the lower limit subtracts PAD_H from the screen height.

Back in UPDATE_PLAY, Rally compares each paddle’s old and new Y positions and divides by DT. That gives its vertical velocity. Keep that number handy: a moving paddle is about to give the ball some spin.

25
INSIDE RALLY · BALL AND COLLISIONS

DON’T SKIP THE PADDLE.

A fast ball can cross a thin paddle between pictures. MOVE_BALL deals with this by splitting a frame into smaller steps, aiming for about six pixels of travel per step and allowing at most eight steps.

5030   SPEED=MAX(1,SQR(BALL_VX*BALL_VX+BALL_VY*BALL_VY))
5040   STEPS=CLAMP(-INT(-SPEED*DT/6),1,8)
5050   SDT=DT/STEPS

The square root measures velocity’s length. -INT(-…) rounds the positive step count up. Each step advances the ball by velocity times SDT, while estimating where the moving paddles were partway through the frame.

Before moving, line 5100 saves the ball’s centre as X0,Y0. FNHITS_PADDLE, defined at line 5710, checks the segment from that old centre to the new one against a rectangle expanded by half a ball on every side.

Picture the ball’s centre travelling through a slightly fatter paddle. This checks its path, not just its landing spot. Direction checks also make sure a ball moving away does not hit the same paddle again.

BOUNCE_OFF_WALLS mirrors any overshoot back into the court and points the vertical velocity inward. Paddle hits place the ball just outside the paddle, call BOUNCE, and play the appropriate hit sound.

INSIDE RALLY · ANGLES AND SPIN

PUT A LITTLE ENGLISH ON IT.

BOUNCE, at line 5510, turns a collision into the next shot. First it adds SPEED_UP to BALL_SPEED, stopping at TOP_SPEED. A long rally gets livelier, but there is a speed limit.

5540   ANGLE=CLAMP((BALL_Y+BALL/2-PAD_Y)/PAD_H-0.5,-0.5,0.5)*1.1
5550   BALL_VX=DIR*BALL_SPEED*COS(ANGLE)
5560   BALL_VY=BALL_SPEED*SIN(ANGLE)+PAD_VY*0.25
